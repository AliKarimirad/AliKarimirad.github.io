Job Market Paper

Cheap Liquidity, Costly Default: Trade Credit in Production Networks

with Khalil Esmkhani · 2026

Firms pay for intermediate inputs either with cash up front or with credit from their suppliers. We quantify what this financing costs the economy in a production-network model with both margins, disciplined by sectoral, balance-sheet and credit data. When liquidity is lent domestically, its interest is a transfer and the working-capital distortion costs little; crisis event studies for emerging markets show that financing becomes costly when the lender moves offshore. The cost of credit comes instead from what default destroys. In the United States, defaultable trade credit costs 0.289% of consumption, almost all of it resources lost in bankruptcy and carried through the network, while misallocation is negligible. Holding the financial calibration fixed and swapping only the input–output structure across five countries, the country with the lowest failure rate bears the largest loss.

Presented at: WEAI 101st Annual Conference, Denver (2026); Network Science in Economics, University of Miami (2026); 8th International Conference on Econometrics and Statistics, Tokyo (2025); 57th Annual Conference of the Canadian Economics Association (2023)

Nonlinear Aggregation in Distorted Economies

with Alejandro Rojas-Bernal · 2026
Abstract

We derive a second-order approximation to real GDP for changes in productivity, price–cost wedges, and wasted distortions around a distorted production-network equilibrium. The central contribution is a decomposition of nonlinear aggregation into downstream cost propagation and upstream payment propagation. Shocks not only reweight sectoral cost exposure but also redirect income between factors and profits and recompose final demand, factor intensity, and input sourcing. These state-dependent channels make curvature around distorted equilibria differ from familiar efficient-economy formulas. In two benchmark economies, the second-order approximation sharply reduces error relative to the exact nonlinear equilibrium, especially for large shocks. In an aggregate TFP application, it also outperforms first-order aggregation, with its largest gains near business-cycle troughs and peaks.

Pricing Permanence: Settlement Charges and Refugee Sorting

2026
Abstract

The United Kingdom has proposed a charge of about £10,000 on eligible adult asylum-support recipients, collected above an earnings threshold and linked to settlement. This paper asks how such a charge changes who chooses the UK, using a six-destination Roy-logit model in which refugees differ in general skill, destination-specific skill transfer, employment attachment, and access to alternative routes. Liability concentrates among workers just above the threshold, whose surplus from choosing the UK is smallest. At equal expected revenue, a capped proportional schedule induces less exit from the UK than the fixed charge and shifts the burden toward higher earners.

Chokepoint Dependence and Alternative Infrastructure: Political Integration, Crude Oil, and the Strait of Hormuz

2026
Abstract

Dependence on a trade chokepoint can reflect politically restricted alternatives, not geography and technology alone. This paper develops a network-design model that jointly selects infrastructure and routes under politically integrated access, and applies it to crude-oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz. A closure-oriented system can sustain the full 16.4 million barrels per day of required deliveries; starting from 2024 infrastructure, this requires $31.9 billion of initial regional pipeline, pumping, and export-terminal additions. Political integration therefore permits substantial reductions in chokepoint dependence through costed alternatives.
